When Not to Automate: The Hidden Risk of Automating Broken Processes

Automating a broken process doesn't fix it. It makes the broken version run faster, more consistently, and much harder to notice.

Every automation pitch sounds the same: "we'll save you X hours a week." Almost none of them ask whether the process being automated should still exist.

That gap is where most automation money goes to die. Automating a broken process doesn't fix it. It makes the broken version run faster, more consistently, and far harder to notice — because nobody has to suffer through it manually any more, and manual suffering was the only signal that it was bad.

This article is about the second question, the one that comes before "can this be automated": does this process deserve to survive in its current form at all?

Why manual pain is a feature

A bad manual process generates continuous, distributed complaint. Someone re-types data between two systems and mentions it. Someone chases three approvals and mentions it. The friction is annoying and it is also your only running measurement of how bad the design is.

Automate it as-is and the complaints stop overnight. Nothing improved — you removed the sensor. The process is now:

  • Faster, so it produces more of whatever it produces, including errors.
  • More consistent, so the errors are systematic instead of random, which makes them harder to spot and easier to build on top of.
  • Invisible, because nobody touches it and nobody thinks about it.
  • Load-bearing, because within two quarters other things depend on its output.

That last one is the expensive part. A bad manual process can be changed with a conversation. A bad automated process that three other systems now consume is a migration project.

Manual friction is expensive and honest. Automated friction is cheap and silent. Silent is worse.

Six things not to automate

1. A process nobody can currently describe end to end

If three people give you three different accounts of what happens after step four, you don't have a process — you have a habit with local variations. Automating it freezes one arbitrary version, usually the one belonging to whoever was in the room during requirements gathering. Map first. Half the time the mapping itself removes the need for the automation.

2. A process that only exists because of another broken process

Reconciliation steps are the classic example. A team spends six hours a week matching two reports that disagree. Automating the matching is a real saving and a permanent commitment to the disagreement. Fix the source and the process disappears entirely — that's a 100% saving, not an 80% one.

3. Judgement work wearing a repetitive costume

Some tasks look repetitive from a distance and contain a handful of high-consequence decisions in the middle. Classifying an unusual customer complaint, deciding whether a shipment exception is worth escalating, approving a non-standard discount. Volume looks like repetition. The distribution of consequences is what matters, not the average case.

4. A process about to change for external reasons

New regulation, a system migration, a pricing model change in the next two quarters. Automation built against a spec with a known expiry date is technical debt purchased deliberately.

5. Anything where the exception rate is above roughly 20%

Above that threshold, the exception handling becomes the actual system: you build automation, then build a manual process to catch what it can't do, then a reconciliation between the two. Three processes where there was one. Below 5% exceptions, automation is usually clean; between 5 and 20% it depends on how expensive an exception is; above 20%, redesign before you automate.

6. Anything whose output nobody checks

If no human currently reads the result, automation removes the last opportunity for the error to be noticed. Before automating, ask who looks at the output and what they would do if it were wrong. If the honest answer is "nobody" and "nothing," you're about to industrialise an unmonitored process.

A worked example: the AI manager that didn't happen

We once evaluated replacing a full-time coordinator role with an AI-driven assistant handling estimates, document preparation and logistics coordination. On paper it was a strong case: high volume, repetitive-looking, clearly defined inputs.

What the process map showed:

  • Roughly 80% of the work was genuine repetition — pulling numbers into a template, formatting documents, sending standard follow-ups, chasing status.
  • Around 20% was a small number of judgement calls: which supplier exception to accept, when a client's request needed a different commercial answer, when a delay was worth escalating and to whom.
  • Almost all of the risk lived in that 20%. The repetitive part being wrong caused rework. The judgement part being wrong lost money or lost a client.

So the honest answer wasn't "yes, automate the role." It was: automate the 80% that was pure repetition, leave the 20% exactly where it was, and — importantly — make the automated portion produce a visible queue for the human, so the judgement calls arrive with context instead of being buried.

The saving was smaller than the original pitch and it held up. A full automation would have produced a larger number in the business case and a worse outcome in the second quarter.

A scorecard for deciding

Before building anything, score the process from 0 to 2 on each line. Anything below 7 out of 12 means redesign first.

  • Stability: will this process be materially the same in 18 months? (0 = a change is already scheduled)
  • Definition: can two people independently describe it identically? (0 = accounts diverge after step three)
  • Exception rate: under 5% = 2, 5–20% = 1, over 20% = 0
  • Consequence distribution: is a wrong output cheap to fix? (0 = a wrong output costs a client or a fine)
  • Verification: does someone read the output and could they tell it was wrong? (0 = nobody looks)
  • Necessity: would this process still be needed if the upstream problem were fixed? (0 = it exists only to compensate)

The scorecard's real function is to make one specific conversation happen: the one where somebody has to say out loud that the process only exists because of a decision nobody wants to revisit. That conversation is what the LIDL case in the €500 million article was avoided at scale.

How to automate the part that should be automated

  1. Map the current process by observation, not by interviewWatch it happen or read the logs. What people describe and what people do differ in exactly the places that break automation.
  2. Split repetition from judgement explicitlyDraw the line and get the people who do the work to argue about where it sits. That argument is the requirements document.
  3. Delete before you buildFor each step, ask what happens if it simply stops. Steps that survive that question honestly are usually fewer than the map suggests.
  4. Automate the repetition and route the judgement, with contextThe human should receive a decision with everything needed to make it, not a notification that something needs attention.
  5. Instrument the output before go-liveDefine the signal that tells you it's still working — a sampled check, a volume anomaly alert, a weekly review of exceptions. Automation without a detector is a bet that nothing will change.
  6. Name an owner and a review dateSix months out, someone re-examines whether the process still deserves to exist. Put it in a calendar, not in a document.

When automation is clearly right

None of this is an argument against automation — the same discipline is what makes it work. Automate confidently when the process is stable, describable, low-exception, verifiable, and would still be needed in a well-run version of the company. Under those conditions, the savings are real, they compound, and the maintenance cost stays proportionate. The small business automation guide covers what to do first when that's your situation.

The question worth asking before any automation project isn't "can this be automated." It's: if I automated this exactly as it exists today, would I be proud of what I shipped?

WHAT TO TAKE AWAY

  • Manual friction is the only running measurement of how bad a process design is; automating it as-is removes the sensor, not the problem.
  • Don't automate a process nobody can describe end to end, one that exists only to compensate for another broken process, or judgement work that looks repetitive from a distance.
  • Exception rate is the practical threshold: under 5% is usually clean, over 20% means redesign before automating.
  • The costs missing from every business case are maintenance, exception handling, silent failure, knowledge loss, ownership and model drift.
  • Automate the repetitive 80% and route the judgement 20% to a human with full context rather than a bare notification.
  • Nothing goes live without a named owner and an answer to "how would we know if this stopped being correct?"

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS

What happens if you automate a broken process?

It runs faster, produces errors systematically rather than randomly, and stops generating the complaints that told you it was badly designed. Within a couple of quarters other systems start consuming its output, so fixing it changes from a conversation into a migration project.

How do I know whether a process should be automated?

Score it on six dimensions: stability over the next 18 months, whether two people describe it identically, exception rate, cost of a wrong output, whether anyone verifies the output, and whether the process would still be needed if the upstream problem were fixed. Weak scores mean redesign first.

What exception rate makes automation a bad idea?

As a working rule: under 5% is usually clean, 5–20% depends on how expensive an exception is, and above 20% you will end up building three processes — the automation, the manual fallback, and a reconciliation between them.

Should AI change this calculation?

It changes what is technically automatable, not what is worth automating. AI-based steps also add a cost the classic business case omits: accuracy at launch is not a constant, so you need ongoing sampling and re-evaluation rather than a one-off acceptance test.

What is the biggest hidden cost of automation?

Silent failure. Every other cost is bounded and roughly estimable; a process that stops being correct without announcing it accumulates damage until someone happens to notice. This is why a monitoring answer should be a precondition for go-live.

Is it ever right to automate a process you plan to change?

Rarely, and only when the automation is deliberately disposable and priced as such. Building against a specification with a known expiry date is technical debt purchased on purpose — acceptable if everyone says so out loud, damaging when it's presented as a permanent solution.
