The One-Person Consulting Studio: When Small Beats Big (and When It Doesn't)

"What if you get sick?" is the standard objection to hiring a solo consultant. It's a fair question — and it's usually pointed at the wrong risk.

"What if you get sick?" is the most common objection to hiring a one-person consulting studio instead of an agency. It is a fair question. It is also usually aimed at the wrong risk.

The honest answer: size is not the risk. Diluted accountability is. A small studio has a real continuity problem and no way to hide it. A large firm has a real dilution problem and an excellent way to hide it. Which one is worse depends entirely on the type of work — and this article sets out where each actually wins.

What the question is really asking

Underneath "what if you get sick" are three separate concerns that deserve separate answers:

  1. Delivery risk. Will the work stop if one person is unavailable? Legitimate, and answerable with concrete guarantees rather than reassurance.
  2. Capability risk. Can one person cover everything this project needs? Also legitimate, and often the real reason to choose a firm.
  3. Procurement risk. If this goes wrong, what does the person who approved it say? This is rarely stated out loud and frequently decisive.

The third one explains a great deal of supplier selection. Nobody was ever fired for hiring a large firm, and everyone knows it. That is a real cost borne by the buyer's organisation, not a quality signal about the supplier.

How agency economics actually work

This isn't a criticism, it's arithmetic. A consulting firm with employees needs utilisation, and utilisation shapes staffing:

  • The person who sells is rarely the person who delivers. Senior partners are the sales function; their time is the firm's scarcest and most expensive resource. Your project is staffed by whoever is available at a margin that works.
  • Leverage is the business model. Profit comes from senior oversight over junior execution. That ratio is the firm's economics, not a per-project choice, which is why "more senior involvement" is difficult to buy even when you offer to pay for it.
  • Reassignment is invisible. If the strong person on your account gets moved to a bigger client, you generally aren't told; you receive a transition and a reassurance.
  • Methodology is an asset that must be reused. A firm's frameworks exist so work is repeatable across clients. That is genuinely valuable for common problems and genuinely limiting for unusual ones — a template will get applied to your problem because that's what makes the economics work.
At a large firm, the gap between who convinced you and who does the work is a feature of the business model. At a one-person studio there's no gap, because there's nowhere for one to hide.

Where a one-person studio is genuinely better

  • Non-standard problems. No template exists, so nothing gets templated onto you. This is the whole category of work where a framework produces a wrong answer confidently.
  • Short, decision-shaped engagements. Two to six weeks, one question, a written answer. Firms are structurally bad at these: the overhead of scoping, staffing and QA exceeds the work.
  • Direct transmission. Your context reaches the person analysing it without passing through an account manager and a kickoff deck. Fewer translation layers, fewer wrong assumptions surviving unnoticed.
  • Aligned incentives on scope. A solo consultant with a full pipeline has no reason to extend a project; there is no bench to keep busy. That single fact removes the most common source of scope inflation.
  • Willingness to say no. Turning down work costs a firm a utilisation slot and costs an individual nothing but the fee. It is much easier to hear "you don't need this project" from someone with no bench.

Where a one-person studio is genuinely worse

Any article that skips this section is marketing. Pick a firm when:

  • The work needs parallel capacity. Forty interviews across six countries in three weeks is a staffing problem, and one person cannot solve it by being clever.
  • It needs several deep specialisms at once. Tax, employment law and systems architecture in one engagement is a team, not a generalist.
  • Continuity over years is the point. A multi-year programme needs institutional memory that survives an individual's life events.
  • Procurement or regulation requires it. Insurance minimums, supplier audits, ISO certification, panel agreements. These are real constraints, not snobbery, and arguing with them wastes everyone's time.
  • You need the name. Sometimes the deliverable's job is to be credible to a board or a regulator. That's a legitimate requirement — just be honest that you're buying signalling, and price it as such.

Answering the continuity question properly

"I'll be fine" is not an answer. A solo consultant who takes the objection seriously should be able to offer four things, and a buyer should ask for them explicitly:

  1. Work stored where you can reach itDeliverables and working material in your systems or a shared repository from day one, not delivered at the end. If the studio disappears mid-project, you keep everything produced so far.
  2. Interim deliverables on a fixed cadenceSomething reviewable every week or two, not a single delivery at the end. This caps your exposure to the length of one cadence instead of the length of the project.
  3. A named fallbackA specific person or firm who could take over, named in advance, ideally who has seen the material. Not a promise to find someone.
  4. Explicit unavailability termsWhat happens to the timeline and the fee if the consultant is unavailable for two weeks. In writing, agreed before starting.
Ask a large firm the equivalent question: "If the person you're putting on this leaves the firm, what changes for me, and who tells me?" The answer is often less concrete than a good solo consultant's — and far less binding.

Five questions that work on either kind of supplier

  1. Who exactly does the work, and are they on this call? Ask for names and the percentage of their time. Write the answer down; it becomes a reference point later.
  2. How many clients will you have in parallel with mine? Both answers can be fine. Evasion is the signal, not the number.
  3. What would make you tell me not to do this project? Anyone who cannot produce a scenario is selling activity.
  4. What do I own at the end, and in what format? A PDF is not ownership. Models, data, scripts and reasoning are.
  5. What happens if the person doing this becomes unavailable? The concrete answer beats the reassuring one, regardless of supplier size.

The full version of this checklist, including the questions that expose padded scopes, is in how to choose a business consultant.

The actual trade

A large firm sells you redundancy and buys back your access to the person who understands your problem. A one-person studio sells you undiluted access and asks you to accept concentration risk. Both are real trades. Neither is free.

The mistake is treating firm size as a proxy for quality in either direction. Small is not automatically more careful, and big is not automatically safer — it is safer for the buyer's career and riskier for the buyer's problem.

So the question worth asking isn't "what if you get sick." It's: would I rather have 20% of a senior person's attention with institutional backup, or 100% of one person's attention with named fallback arrangements? For a bounded, non-standard, decision-shaped problem, the second answer wins more often than procurement processes allow.

WHAT TO TAKE AWAY

  • Firm size is not a quality signal in either direction: big is safer for the buyer's career, small is often safer for the buyer's problem.
  • Agency leverage — senior sells, junior delivers — is the business model, which is why more senior time is hard to buy at any price.
  • Choose a firm for parallel capacity, multiple deep specialisms, multi-year continuity, regulated procurement, or when you're deliberately buying a name.
  • Choose a one-person studio for bounded, non-standard, decision-shaped work where a template would produce a confident wrong answer.
  • Demand four continuity guarantees from a solo consultant: work in your systems, interim deliverables, a named fallback, and written unavailability terms.
  • Ask both kinds of supplier who does the work, what you own at the end, and what would make them advise against the project.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS

Is it risky to hire a one-person consulting studio?

There is a real concentration risk, and it should be managed rather than dismissed. Ask for deliverables stored in your systems from day one, interim outputs every one to two weeks, a named fallback person or firm, and written terms covering unavailability. Those four measures reduce your exposure to roughly one delivery cycle instead of the whole project.

Why would a small studio be better than a large consulting firm?

Because at a firm the person who sells the work is usually not the person who delivers it, and reused methodology is what makes the economics work. For a bounded, non-standard problem that is a disadvantage: a template gets applied confidently to a situation it doesn't fit, and your context passes through translation layers before reaching whoever analyses it.

When should I definitely choose a firm instead?

When the work needs parallel capacity across locations, several deep specialisms simultaneously, continuity over several years, or when procurement, insurance or regulatory requirements make an individual supplier impossible. Also when the deliverable's job is to carry a recognisable name to a board or regulator.

How do I check whether a firm will actually staff senior people on my project?

Ask for names and the percentage of their time on your engagement, in writing, and ask what happens if that person leaves or is reassigned, and who informs you. Vagueness here is the signal. Utilisation pressure means reassignment is normal and rarely announced.

Does a solo consultant cost less than an agency?

The day rate is often comparable; the total is usually lower because there is no account-management layer, no internal QA overhead and less scope inflation. The economics differ more than the rate does — a consultant without a bench to keep busy has no structural reason to extend a project.

What should I own when the engagement ends?

Not just a PDF. Ask for the underlying models, data, scripts, and the written reasoning behind recommendations, in editable formats, in your own systems. If the only artefact is a presentation, you bought a performance rather than an asset.
