How to Choose a Business Consultant: 9 Questions That Separate Expertise From a Good Deck

Selection usually fails in the first conversation, not in delivery. These nine questions surface in thirty minutes what a reference call takes weeks to reveal.

Most bad consulting engagements were decided in the first thirty-minute call, not during delivery. The selection signal is available early — it's just drowned out by rapport, references and a well-produced deck, all three of which are easy to manufacture.

What follows is nine questions that are hard to answer well without actually having done the work. For each one, what a good answer sounds like and what a bad one sounds like. You are not testing knowledge. You are testing whether their judgement survives contact with a specific situation — yours.

What you're actually selecting for

Three properties predict outcomes better than experience, credentials or firm size:

  1. Willingness to narrow. Good consultants make your problem smaller and more specific. Selling makes it bigger.
  2. Willingness to be wrong in public. Someone who states in advance what would falsify their recommendation is doing analysis. Someone who doesn't is performing.
  3. Willingness to lose the deal. The ability to say "you don't need this" is the single most reliable quality signal, and it's structurally easier for suppliers without a bench to fill.

The nine questions

What to ask, and how to read the answer
QuestionGood answer sounds likeBad answer sounds like
1. What would make you tell me not to do this project?A specific scenario: "If your data isn't in one place yet, this analysis will cost twice what it's worth — fix that first.""There's always value in a fresh perspective."
2. What's the smallest version of this engagement that would still be useful?An actual smaller option, priced, with what you'd lose by taking it.Reframing the question into why the full scope is necessary.
3. Walk me through a project that didn't work. What did you get wrong?A named wrong assumption and what they'd change in their own method."The client wasn't ready" / "leadership didn't support it."
4. Who does the work, and what percentage of their time is on this?Names, percentages, and what happens if that changes."Our senior team oversees all engagements."
5. What do I own at the end, and in what format?Editable models, data, scripts, written reasoning — in your systems.A final presentation and a report.
6. How will we know in six months whether this worked?A metric that exists today, its current value, and the expected direction."You'll see improved alignment and efficiency."
7. What do you need from us, and what happens if we don't provide it?A concrete list with consequences: "Without the ticket export, weeks 2–3 become estimates.""Just general availability from your team."
8. What's your view on our problem right now, before any work?A tentative hypothesis with stated uncertainty — and a question back.Refusal to speculate, or total confidence with no data.
9. What happens if you're unavailable for two weeks?A named fallback, interim deliverables, work stored in your systems.Reassurance without mechanism.

Question 8 is the one that most reliably separates people. A consultant with real experience in your problem type will offer a hypothesis within minutes and immediately name what would change their mind. Total refusal to speculate usually means no pattern library; total certainty usually means a template is already being selected.

You are not buying answers. You are buying the quality of the reasoning that will produce them — and reasoning is visible in how someone handles a question they can't yet answer.

Red flags in the proposal

  • "Analysis and support" as a deliverable. No definition of done, therefore no upper bound.
  • No breakdown of days by phase. Nothing to negotiate but total price.
  • Unnamed senior resources. "A senior consultant" is a role, not a commitment.
  • Findings promised before discovery. If the conclusion is in the proposal, you're buying a template.
  • Methodology as the main selling point. Frameworks are how firms make repeat work profitable. That is fine for common problems and a warning sign for unusual ones.
  • No change-request definition. Everything unlisted becomes one.
  • Guaranteed outcomes. Anyone guaranteeing a business result before diagnosis is either uninformed or selling.
  • Scope that covers every department. Breadth is how a report avoids naming a responsible executive — see the audit nobody reads.

References: ask the right three questions

Reference calls are usually theatre, because the consultant chooses the referee. Three questions still extract signal:

  1. "What did you have to do that you didn't expect?" Reveals how much of the load actually landed on the client, which is the most commonly understated cost.
  2. "What did they get wrong, and how did you find out?" Every real engagement contains a wrong turn. A referee who can't name one either didn't engage deeply or is being careful.
  3. "Would you use them for a different type of problem?" A hesitant yes tells you the boundary of their competence, which is more useful than an enthusiastic one.

If possible, ask for one reference from an engagement that ended early or didn't get renewed. Refusal is not disqualifying, but a supplier who can offer one is telling you something real.

Structure the first engagement to be cheap to exit

  1. Buy a paid diagnostic firstA short, priced piece of work that ends in a scoped plan. You're buying an estimate rather than guessing at one, and you get to watch them work before committing.
  2. Require an interim deliverable inside two weeksWhatever it is, it should be reviewable. This caps your exposure and gives you a real basis for the continue-or-stop decision.
  3. Agree the stop point in writing"At the end of phase one we decide whether to proceed; no notice period, no assumption of continuation." Easy to agree at the start, awkward to introduce later.
  4. Keep the deliverables in your systemsFrom day one, not at handover. If the relationship ends, you keep everything produced.
  5. Name your own ownerSomeone internal who is accountable for acting on the output. An engagement with no internal owner produces a document, regardless of who you hired.
If a supplier resists a paid diagnostic with a genuine stop point, that's the answer to question 1 in a different form: they are unwilling to be evaluated on a small piece before the large one.

One practical sequence

For a bounded problem, this takes about a week: write the question in one sentence; send it to three suppliers of deliberately different shapes (an independent, a boutique, a larger firm — the trade-offs are laid out in this comparison); run the nine questions in a 30-minute call each; compare the three proposals structurally rather than on price, using the pricing factors in what consulting actually costs; buy a diagnostic from one.

The point of talking to three deliberately different suppliers isn't to create competition on price. It's that their disagreements about your problem are free information — and often more valuable than any single proposal.

WHAT TO TAKE AWAY

  • Three properties predict outcomes better than credentials: willingness to narrow the problem, to state what would falsify their recommendation, and to lose the deal.
  • The most diagnostic question is "what's your view on our problem right now?" — a hypothesis with stated uncertainty beats both refusal and certainty.
  • Proposal red flags: "analysis and support" as a deliverable, unnamed senior resources, findings promised before discovery, and scope covering every department.
  • On reference calls ask what the client had to do unexpectedly, what the consultant got wrong, and whether they'd use them for a different problem type.
  • Prior experience of your specific failure mode and published writing are high-signal; logos, degrees and awards are not.
  • De-risk the first engagement: paid diagnostic, interim deliverable within two weeks, written stop point, deliverables in your systems, and a named internal owner.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS

What is the single best question to ask a consultant before hiring them?

"What would make you tell me not to do this project?" A specific scenario means they have a real threshold for usefulness. "There's always value in a fresh perspective" means they have none, and you will be sold whatever you ask for.

How do I check a consultant's experience without relying on client logos?

Ask them to describe the mechanism of a failure they have seen in your problem type — not the client, the mechanism. Someone who has been there will describe how the thing went wrong step by step. Published writing on the topic is also cheap to verify and hard to fake over time.

Should I ask for references, and what should I ask them?

Yes, while remembering the consultant chose them. Ask what the client had to do that they didn't expect, what the consultant got wrong and how it was discovered, and whether they'd use them for a different type of problem. If possible, request a reference from an engagement that ended early.

What are the biggest red flags in a consulting proposal?

"Analysis and support" as the deliverable, no breakdown of days by phase, unnamed senior resources, conclusions stated before any discovery, guaranteed business outcomes, no definition of what triggers a change request, and a scope that covers every department.

How can I reduce the risk of hiring the wrong consultant?

Buy a small paid diagnostic first with a written stop point, require a reviewable interim deliverable within two weeks, keep all work in your own systems from day one, and appoint an internal owner accountable for acting on the output.

Should I talk to more than one consultant?

Talk to three of deliberately different shapes — an independent, a boutique and a larger firm. Not to create price competition, but because their disagreements about your problem are free diagnostic information, often more valuable than any single proposal.
