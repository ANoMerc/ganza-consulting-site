Why Your Multilingual Website Isn't Converting (It's Not About Translation)

Translation is the easy 20% of localisation. The 80% that decides whether a site converts has almost nothing to do with words.

Most companies localise a website by translating it. That is the easy 20% of the problem, and it's the part everyone gets right.

The other 80% — the part that decides whether a visitor from that market actually buys — is layout, trust signals, transactional expectations and search visibility. A site can read perfectly in a language and still feel imported, and visitors rarely articulate why. They just leave faster.

Here is what actually breaks, in the order it typically costs you money.

1. The layout was designed around one language's string lengths

Text length changes substantially between languages. German and Russian expand relative to English; Chinese and Japanese contract; Finnish produces long unbreakable compounds. Designs built to fit English exactly will fail somewhere.

Where it shows first:

  • Navigation. A five-item menu that fits English wraps to two lines in German and pushes the CTA below the fold on a laptop.
  • Buttons. Fixed-width buttons truncate or the label wraps mid-word. The primary CTA is the single worst place for this and the most common one.
  • Form labels and validation messages. Usually written last, translated last, and tested never — and they sit directly in the conversion path.
  • Tables and pricing grids. Column widths tuned to short English headers become unreadable with longer ones.

The fix is structural, not linguistic: fluid widths, no fixed-height containers around text, and a test pass with the longest translation you actually have. A pragmatic rule is to design so that any string can grow by about 35% without breaking the layout.

2. Reading direction and scanning patterns

Right-to-left languages (Arabic, Hebrew, Farsi, Urdu) are not a mirror-image switch. Direction reverses for text flow, layout order and navigation, but numbers, Latin brand names, code, phone numbers and many charts stay left-to-right. Icons with directional meaning — back arrows, progress indicators, sliders — need to flip; logos and clock icons don't.

Beyond RTL, scanning behaviour differs even between left-to-right markets: how far a reader trusts a page before scrolling, how much density reads as "serious" versus "cluttered", and whether a wall of text signals credibility or carelessness. A layout that feels confident and airy in one market can read as thin and unserious in another.

The test isn't "does it read correctly in this language." It's "would someone who only reads this language assume it was built by someone who only reads this language."

3. Trust signals don't transfer

This is the largest and least technical cause of lost conversion. Every market has its own evidence of legitimacy, and a translated page usually carries over the source market's set — which reads as nothing at all.

None of this appears in a translation brief, and all of it is inside the conversion path.

4. Transactional expectations

The checkout or contact step is where imported assumptions become abandonment:

  • Payment methods. Card-only checkout is normal in some markets and a hard stop in others where bank transfer, local wallets or invoice-on-delivery dominate.
  • Address forms. A form that requires a state and a five-digit ZIP breaks for most of the world. So does forcing a first name / last name split.
  • Currency and tax display. Whether prices are shown with or without VAT is a market convention; getting it wrong makes you look either expensive or evasive.
  • Formats. Dates (12/06 means two different days), decimal separators, phone formats, name order. Each one is a small credibility leak in the exact moment you're asking for money.
  • Working hours and response promises. "We reply within 24 hours" means something different across a time-zone gap, and unmet timing promises cost more than vague ones.

5. Search engines can only see one version of your site

This is the failure that hides all the others: you fixed the site, and half of it isn't being indexed. The most common technical mistakes:

  1. Client-side language switching only. If both languages live in one URL and JavaScript toggles them, search engines index one version. The other language effectively doesn't exist as a landing page.
  2. Missing or non-reciprocal hreflang. Every language version must point to every other one and to itself, with matching URLs on both sides. One-way declarations are ignored.
  3. Wrong or invented language codes. Use ISO 639-1 language codes plus optional ISO 3166-1 Alpha-2 region codes. en-GB is valid; en-UK is not.
  4. No x-default. Declare which version serves users whose language you don't target.
  5. Canonical fighting hreflang. Each language version should be canonical to itself. Pointing every localised page's canonical at the English one removes them from the index — a surprisingly common configuration.
  6. Automatic IP-based redirects. Forcing a visitor (or a crawler) into a language version by geography prevents indexing of the others and irritates people who deliberately chose a language.
Practical structure that avoids most of this: one physical URL per language (/ and /ru/, or subdomains, or ccTLDs), reciprocal hreflang tags including x-default, self-referencing canonicals, and a language switch that navigates to a real URL rather than silently swapping text. Let users choose; never redirect them by IP.

6. The content strategy is a translation, not a market

The deepest version of the problem: the page answers the questions your home market asks. Objections differ by market — price sensitivity, contract length, data residency, whether working with a foreign supplier is normal or exotic. A word-perfect translation of the wrong objection handling converts worse than clumsy copy addressing the right one.

The same applies to search intent. Direct translation of keywords frequently misses how the market actually searches: different phrasing, different level of technicality, sometimes an English loanword that ranks better than the native term. Keyword research has to be redone per language, not translated.

A localisation checklist that catches most of it

  • Every string can grow 35% without breaking the layout; longest-language pass completed on nav, buttons, forms and tables.
  • Directional icons flip for RTL; numbers, brand names and code stay LTR.
  • Footer discloses at the level local competitors do.
  • The primary contact channel is the market's default, not yours.
  • Payment methods and address form match local reality; date and number formats localised.
  • One indexable URL per language, reciprocal hreflang including x-default, self-referencing canonical.
  • No IP-based forced redirects; language switch changes the URL.
  • Keyword research redone natively, not translated.
  • Error and validation messages translated and visually tested.
  • A native speaker who is also a buyer in that market has reviewed the conversion path — not just the text.

How to sequence the work

  1. Fix indexing firstNothing else matters if the market can't find the page. URLs, hreflang, canonicals and the switch behaviour are a few days of work with the largest single effect.
  2. Then fix the conversion pathContact method, form, payment, formats. Narrow, measurable, and where the money is lost fastest.
  3. Then fix trust signalsFooter disclosure, proof, certifications. Cheap to change, and it compounds with the previous step.
  4. Then re-do content per marketObjection handling and keyword targeting. Most expensive, slowest to pay back, and pointless before the first three are done.

Most localisation budgets run this list backwards: they start with content and never reach hreflang. If you only have budget for one step, do the first — a correctly indexed mediocre translation outperforms a beautiful one nobody can find.

WHAT TO TAKE AWAY

  • Translation is roughly 20% of localisation; layout tolerance, trust signals, transactional expectations and indexing are the rest.
  • Design so any string can grow ~35% without breaking, and test with your longest language — buttons and form messages fail first.
  • Trust signals are market-specific: match local competitors' level of disclosure and lead with the market's default contact channel.
  • The most expensive technical mistake is client-side-only language switching, followed by non-reciprocal hreflang and canonicals pointing at the source language.
  • Never redirect by IP; let the user choose and make the switch change the URL.
  • Sequence the work indexing → conversion path → trust signals → content. Most budgets run it backwards.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS

Why isn't my translated website converting?

Usually because translation was the only thing that changed. The layout still assumes one language's string lengths, the trust signals are the source market's, the contact and payment options are the ones you use at home, and often half the site isn't indexed because the language switch happens only in the browser.

What is the correct hreflang setup for a multilingual site?

One indexable URL per language, reciprocal hreflang annotations where every version references every other version and itself, valid ISO 639-1 language codes with optional ISO 3166-1 Alpha-2 region codes, an x-default entry, and a self-referencing canonical on each language version.

Should I redirect visitors to their language automatically by IP?

No. It prevents search engines from indexing the versions they are not served, and it overrides deliberate user choices. Detect and offer — a dismissible suggestion to switch — but let the user decide, and make the switch change the URL.

How much extra space should a design leave for translation?

As a working rule, any string should be able to grow by about 35% without breaking the layout. Test with the longest language you actually publish, focusing on navigation, primary buttons, form labels, validation messages and table headers.

Do I need to redo keyword research for each language?

Yes. Direct translation of keywords regularly misses how a market searches — different phrasing, a different level of technical vocabulary, and sometimes an English loanword that outperforms the native term. Research natively per language rather than translating a keyword list.

What should I fix first if the budget only covers one thing?

Indexing: URLs, hreflang, canonicals and the language-switch behaviour. A correctly indexed mediocre translation outperforms an excellent one that search engines cannot see.
