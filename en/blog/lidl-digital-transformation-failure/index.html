How Lidl Lost €500 Million: A Case Study in Digital Transformation Failure

The eLWIS project didn't fail in a single moment. It drifted for seven years — until keeping it alive cost more than admitting it was dead.

In 2011 Lidl started eLWIS — an SAP for Retail programme meant to replace its in-house merchandise management system across a business operating in around 30 countries. In 2018 the company cancelled it and went back to the old system. Publicly reported cost: roughly €500 million.

The short version of what went wrong is not "SAP is bad" and not "Lidl was incompetent." It is this: the company chose to bend the software around one existing process rather than change the process, and then spent seven years paying compound interest on that decision while its reporting stayed calm.

That combination — a customisation taken to protect a competitive advantage, plus a governance layer that couldn't see the cost accumulating — is the most common way large transformations die. Below is what actually happened, why each step looked reasonable at the time, and the five checks that would have surfaced it years earlier.

The timeline, briefly

Lidl eLWIS — publicly reported milestones
PeriodWhat happened
2011Programme starts. Goal: replace the legacy in-house merchandise management system with SAP for Retail across the group.
2011–2016Multi-year build with heavy customisation. Deadlines move. Scope stays.
~2017Limited rollouts reported in smaller markets — Austria, Northern Ireland, the United States. CEO Sven Seidel leaves after less than three years; senior IT leadership changes around the same time.
2018Programme cancelled. Lidl returns to the legacy system. Reported spend ≈ €500 million.

Note the shape of that table. There is no disaster in it. No outage, no breach, no single catastrophic go-live. The failure is entirely made of time.

The decision that set the direction: inventory valuation

The widely reported technical root is unglamorous. Lidl valued inventory at purchase price. SAP's retail standard is built around retail price valuation. Two defensible accounting conventions, incompatible defaults.

Lidl had a real reason to keep its convention: purchase-price logic was woven into how the company negotiated, priced and measured itself, and leadership was unwilling to give up something it treated as a competitive edge. So the decision was made to adapt the software to the process instead of the process to the software.

Read in isolation, that is a reasonable business call. The problem is what it implies structurally:

  • A core valuation change is not a setting. It reaches into procurement, replenishment, pricing, store operations and reporting — so the customisation is not one change, it is a permanent branch line running through the whole system.
  • Every standard-product update now has to be re-reconciled against that branch. Upgrade cost stops being an event and becomes a running tax.
  • The system can no longer be tested against the vendor's reference behaviour, because it no longer behaves like the reference. You lose the cheapest source of truth you had.
  • The people who understand the branch become a bottleneck — and, over seven years, they change jobs.
Customisation is not a cost. It is a subscription, and nobody puts the renewal price on the business case.

This is the general rule the case illustrates: the expensive part of a customisation is never the customisation. It is everything that afterwards has to be reconciled with it, forever.

Why nobody stopped it

Seven years is a long time to not stop something. It is worth being precise about why that happens, because the mechanism is the transferable part.

1. Every increment was locally rational

No one ever approved "spend €500m and cancel." They approved a sequence of small, sensible-looking things: this integration, that reconciliation, one more quarter of stabilisation. Each was cheaper than stopping. That is the defining property of drift — at every single decision point, continuing is the cheaper option, right up until the whole thing is written off.

2. Reporting measured activity, not viability

Status reporting on programmes like this tracks deliverables completed, tickets closed, modules configured, milestones passed. All of those can be green while the answer to "will this system run our largest markets at real volume?" is quietly getting worse. Deployments reportedly worked in smaller markets; the larger, higher-intensity ones were where the customised design ran out of road.

3. The escalation path had a filter on it

Engineers raised architecture and scalability concerns. The gap between what dashboards showed and what practitioners saw kept widening. Signals that survive four layers of "let's not escalate that yet" arrive as adjectives, not as decisions. This is the same structural problem described in infrastructure inbreeding: when the layer raising the alarm and the layer receiving it share assumptions and incentives, the alarm becomes noise.

4. Sunk cost had a face

By year five, cancelling meant a named executive publicly owning a nine-figure write-off. The CEO left in 2017 amid reported strategy disagreements; senior IT leadership turned over. It is notably common that a programme becomes cancellable only after the people identified with it are gone — which is an organisational fact, not a technical one.

What Lidl actually got right

Two things, and skipping them would make this a morality tale rather than a case study.

  • They stopped. Writing off €500 million and returning to the legacy system was the correct decision, made late. Plenty of comparable programmes never reach that decision at all and keep bleeding.
  • They kept the fallback alive. The old in-house system still worked, which is the only reason cancellation was survivable. Retiring your fallback before the replacement has proven itself at full volume is how a project failure becomes an operational crisis.

Five checks that catch this pattern early

None of these require a consultant, a new tool, or a re-planning exercise. All of them can be run on an existing programme this month.

  1. Write down every deviation from standard, with its permanent ownerNot a list of change requests — a list of behaviours that will differ from the vendor's product forever. If that list has more than a handful of entries touching core data models, you have chosen a branch line, whether or not anyone said so out loud.
  2. Price the second upgrade, not the firstAsk what it will cost to apply the vendor's next two major releases on top of your deviations. If nobody can answer, that number is unbounded and it is already in your budget.
  3. Define the kill criteria before the next milestone, in writing"If by date X we cannot process the volume of our three largest markets in a production-equivalent test, we stop and reassess." Written before the date, by the person who will be in the room after it. Kill criteria written after a slip are negotiations, not criteria.
  4. Test where it actually hurts, not where it's convenientPilots in small markets are useful for change management and useless for capacity proof. Whatever your equivalent of "largest, highest-intensity market" is, that is the only pilot that answers the viability question.
  5. Ask the practitioners directly, once per quarter, off the reporting lineOne question: "What would you tell me if there were no consequences?" Asked by someone senior enough to act and structured so the answer doesn't route through the layer being evaluated. This is the cheapest early-warning system that exists and almost nobody runs it.
A useful rule of thumb: if your programme cannot name a condition under which it would be stopped, it is not a project — it is a commitment. Commitments do not get cancelled; they get written off.

The transferable version, for a company that isn't Lidl

Most readers are not rolling out SAP across 30 countries. The mechanism scales down perfectly:

  • You buy a CRM and configure it around your existing sales stages instead of examining whether those stages are right. Two years later, every integration has an exception for you.
  • You automate an approval chain exactly as it exists, because changing it would require a conversation with a department head. Now the awkward step is faster, permanent and invisible — the failure mode described in when not to automate.
  • You commission a website rebuild that reproduces the current structure because "users are used to it," then spend the next three years working around a navigation nobody defends.

In every version, the same trade happens: a short, uncomfortable conversation about changing a process is avoided, and its cost is converted into a long, quiet stream of technical debt that never appears as a line item.

Large transformations rarely break from one bad decision. They break from the accumulated distance between what is actually happening and what people feel safe saying out loud — and that distance is measurable, if anyone is willing to ask.

WHAT TO TAKE AWAY

  • The failure was made of time, not of a single catastrophic event: seven years of locally rational decisions, each cheaper than stopping.
  • A customisation to core logic (purchase-price vs retail-price valuation) is not a cost, it is a permanent tax on every future upgrade.
  • Status reporting measured activity while viability quietly degraded; small-market pilots proved change management, not capacity.
  • Write kill criteria before the milestone, not after the slip — otherwise they are negotiations.
  • Keep the fallback system alive until the replacement has proven itself at full production volume.
  • Ask practitioners one consequence-free question per quarter, off the normal reporting line. It is the cheapest early-warning system there is.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS

Why did Lidl's SAP implementation fail?

The most widely reported root cause is that Lidl valued inventory at purchase price while SAP's retail standard is built around retail-price valuation. Rather than change the process, Lidl customised the software. That deviation touched procurement, pricing, replenishment and reporting, made every future upgrade more expensive, and combined with reporting that tracked activity rather than viability, allowed the programme to drift for seven years.

How much did the Lidl eLWIS project cost?

Approximately €500 million (some sources quote a figure closer to $580–600 million) over roughly seven years, from the 2011 start to the 2018 cancellation, after which Lidl returned to its legacy in-house merchandise management system.

Was this SAP's fault?

No. The system behaved as designed; the conflict was between a standard product's assumptions and a business process the company chose not to change. The same outcome is reachable with any major platform. The decision to customise rather than adapt is where the risk was created.

What is the single best early-warning check for a programme like this?

Written kill criteria agreed before the next milestone: a specific, measurable condition under which the programme stops. If a programme cannot name such a condition, it is a commitment rather than a project, and commitments get written off rather than cancelled.

Does this only apply to large enterprises?

No. The same mechanism appears at any scale: configuring a CRM around unexamined sales stages, automating an approval chain exactly as it exists, or rebuilding a website on a structure nobody defends. In each case a short uncomfortable process conversation is exchanged for a long quiet stream of technical debt.

When is customising standard software actually justified?

When the deviation is genuinely the source of competitive advantage, is isolated from core data models, has a named permanent owner, and the cost of applying the vendor's next two major releases on top of it has been estimated and accepted. If any of those four is missing, the customisation is being bought on credit.
